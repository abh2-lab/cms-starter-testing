The Concrete Lungs: How Old Chimneys Define New Skylines

KA
By Kabir Architect March 2, 2026 8 min read Heritage

The smokestacks of Mumbai's former textile mills rise above the city like fossilized vertebrae of a once-living industrial giant. These chimneys — crumbling, soot-stained, impossibly tall — are among the last physical reminders of an era when Mumbai was known as "The Manchester of the East."

Today, they stand surrounded by gleaming glass towers, artisanal coffee shops, and co-working spaces. The juxtaposition is jarring: a 150-foot brick chimney casting its shadow over a luxury apartment's infinity pool. But for the communities who lived and worked in these mill compounds, these structures represent something far more profound than architectural curiosity.

The History Beneath the Concrete

Mumbai's textile mills, concentrated in the belt stretching from Byculla to Worli, were once the economic backbone of the city. At their peak in the 1970s and 1980s, over 250,000 workers operated more than 80 mills across central Mumbai. These were not just factories — they were complete ecosystems with their own housing colonies, medical facilities, schools, and social clubs.

"When you walked through the mill compound at shift change, the ground would vibrate. The air tasted of raw cotton. You could hear the looms from two streets away. That world is gone — but the chimneys remember."

— Rekha Deshpande, former mill worker, Parel

The Great Bombay Textile Strike of 1982, led by Dr. Datta Samant, marked the beginning of the end. Over 250,000 workers walked out demanding better wages and working conditions. The strike lasted 18 months and resulted in the permanent closure of dozens of mills. The land beneath them — prime real estate in the heart of South Mumbai — became the most contested territory in the city's modern history.

Gentrification in Real Time

Walking through Lower Parel today, it's difficult to imagine the landscape of just three decades ago. Where the Phoenix Mills once housed thousands of textile workers, a massive shopping mall now attracts weekend crowds. Where the Dawn Mills processed raw cotton, high-rise residential towers sell flats at prices that would stagger even Mumbai's inflation-hardened residents.

But the transformation hasn't been uniform. Pockets of the old mill culture survive — sometimes literally in the shadow of new construction:

  • Worker housing colonies — many still operational, though increasingly pressured by developers
  • Mill chimneys — protected under heritage regulations but often poorly maintained
  • Community gathering spaces — temples, gymnasiums, and social clubs that predate independence
  • Oral histories — preserved by dedicated community organizations and citizen journalists

The Preservation Paradox

Heritage conservationists face a unique challenge in Mumbai's mill districts. Unlike European industrial heritage sites — which are often repurposed as museums or cultural centres — Mumbai's mills sit on some of the most valuable real estate on Earth. The economic pressure to demolish and rebuild is immense.

Under the Development Control Regulations of 1991 (later amended), one-third of mill land was reserved for open public spaces, one-third for the Maharashtra Housing and Area Development Authority (MHADA) for affordable housing, and the remaining third could be used for commercial development. In practice, the implementation has been uneven.

"Preservation isn't just about bricks and mortar. It's about preserving the memory of what happened here — the labour, the struggle, the community. A chimney without its story is just a pillar."

— Prof. Neera Adarkar, architectural historian

What Comes Next

Several recent initiatives offer hope. The Mumbai Heritage Conservation Committee has identified 56 mill structures worthy of preservation. Community organizations have begun digital archiving projects, collecting photographs, oral histories, and documents from former mill workers and their families.

Mill-Times itself was born from this impulse — the recognition that these stories, if not documented now, will be lost forever. Our citizen journalists are not just reporters; they are custodians of a living history that official archives have largely overlooked.

The chimneys will continue to define Mumbai's skyline for decades to come. Whether they stand as monuments to a forgotten past or as living connections to the city's industrial soul depends on the stories we choose to tell — and the voices we choose to amplify.
